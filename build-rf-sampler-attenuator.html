DIY TEST EQUIPMENT

Build a low-cost RF sampler/attenuator for AM transmitter testing

Published September 27, 2026 · Resonance Radio

A cheap SDR becomes much more useful as a transmitter instrument when the RF first passes through a known 50 Ω load, a large attenuation network and a DC block.

What this build is for

This measurement head is intended for low-power AM transmitters around the medium-wave band. It gives an RTL-SDR a repeatable, protected sample while the transmitter sees an approximately 50 Ω bench load.

Rev B: simplify the attenuator around Amazon-available values

The first design used uncommon E96 shunt values. This revision instead relies on the SDR's nominal 50 Ω input and uses three readily available series resistance values. The result is simpler, cheaper and easy to calibrate in software.

Why the transmitter still sees nearly 50 Ω

The sampler branch is high resistance compared with the dummy load. With the SDR connected, the calculated transmitter-side resistance is about 49.48 Ω on the least-attenuated range, 49.83 Ω in the middle and 49.95 Ω at maximum attenuation. That is close enough for this low-cost characterization head, but it is still a bench load—not proof that an inexpensive short-wire transmitter was designed for a 50 Ω antenna.

Build and test sequence

  1. Mount the two 100 Ω / 1 W resistors in parallel across the RF input with short leads.
  2. Wire the rotary selector to choose 4.7 kΩ, 10 kΩ + 4.7 kΩ in series, or 47 kΩ between the input node and sampled-output path.
  3. Add the 1 µF film capacitor in series before the output SMA.
  4. If desired, place two 1N5711 diodes anti-parallel from the SDR side of the capacitor to ground as an emergency clamp.
  5. Continuity-check the box before connecting the SDR. Verify no DC path exists from the transmitter input to the SDR center pin.
  6. Always begin measurements on the highest-attenuation range, with SDR gain low and the bias tee disabled.
Power limit

Label this Rev B head 1 W MAX CONTINUOUS. The parallel load resistors are nominally 2 W total, but a conservative derating gives thermal margin in a small enclosure. This is not a load for unknown or high-power transmitters.

Calibrate before believing the dB labels

The selector labels are nominal. Feed a known low-level signal through each range, record the measured difference, and store those three calibration offsets in the analyzer software. That corrects resistor tolerance, the SDR's actual input impedance and wiring loss.

A useful second check is attenuation scaling: if a suspicious spur is really from the transmitter, it should move predictably as the external attenuation changes. A spur created by an overloaded SDR often does not. This is one of the main reasons to include three hardware ranges rather than a single fixed divider.

What about the RTL-SDR itself?

The preferred receiver for this project remains the RTL-SDR Blog V4L because it is designed for direct HF coverage. During this parts check I did not find a genuine V4L Amazon listing; Amazon results were returning older V3 units and generic products. I would rather leave the receiver link untagged than label the wrong product as a V4L.

What this enables

With the sampler head plus an SDR, the Resonance Radio analyzer can automate carrier-frequency logging, drift, AM modulation depth, occupied bandwidth, audio hum sidebands and relative harmonic measurements.
