REGULATION + DESIGN

Part 15 AM basics: what the 100 mW and 3 m rules actually mean

Published September 27, 2026 · Resonance Radio

A low-power AM project is much easier to design when the legal constraint is treated as an engineering boundary from the beginning rather than as an afterthought.

The rule we design around

For U.S. operation under 47 CFR §15.219, the total input power to the final RF stage must not exceed 100 mW. The combined length of the transmission line, antenna and ground lead, if used, must not exceed 3 meters. Emissions outside 510–1705 kHz must also be sufficiently suppressed.

Design implication

A transmitter advertised as “500 mW” is not automatically suitable at its maximum setting. The useful question is what the final RF stage is actually consuming and what the antenna system is doing.

Why antenna matching matters so much

A three-meter conductor is electrically very short at medium-wave frequencies. Its radiation resistance is small and its reactance is large. That means the matching network, coil loss, ground arrangement and nearby metal can dominate performance.

Rather than chasing raw transmitter power, a better experiment is to measure supply current to the final stage, tune the antenna network for a repeatable peak, and compare received signal at fixed locations.

Interference still matters

Part 15 devices operate on a non-interference basis. Even a build that meets a power or antenna-length provision should not cause harmful interference. Pick a genuinely unused local channel and inspect the spectrum for harmonics and spurious output before regular use.

A reproducible test record

  1. Record carrier frequency and supply voltage.
  2. Record final-stage DC voltage and current.
  3. Record antenna, feed and ground-lead lengths.
  4. Capture an SDR spectrum at a fixed distance.
  5. Log receiver model, location and signal observations.

Primary reference: FCC §15.219 text. This page is an engineering summary, not legal advice.

Three mistakes that ruin a useful comparison

Changing antenna geometry and transmitter power at the same time makes it impossible to know which change mattered. Using a long ground lead as an accidental radiator defeats the point of a controlled short-antenna experiment. Reporting “range” without naming the receiver, local noise floor and antenna orientation also turns a measurement into an anecdote. A better result is a table of received levels at fixed locations with the entire RF setup described.
