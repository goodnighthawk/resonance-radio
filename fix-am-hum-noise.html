TROUBLESHOOTING

Fixing hum and noise in a low-cost AM transmitter

Published September 27, 2026 · Resonance Radio

Hum is one of the fastest ways to make a small AM station sound cheap. Fortunately, its causes are usually systematic.

First isolate the source

Run the receiver on batteries. Then run the transmitter from a known clean supply with the audio cable disconnected. If the hum remains, investigate the transmitter supply and RF layout. If it disappears, reconnect the audio chain one element at a time.

Common paths

  • Switch-mode power adapter noise.
  • Ground loops between computer and transmitter.
  • USB charger noise entering the audio source.
  • Unshielded audio cable near the RF section.
  • RF feedback into the audio input.

A useful order of attack

  1. Battery-power the laptop or source.
  2. Try a different regulated transmitter supply.
  3. Shorten and shield the audio cable.
  4. Keep RF and audio wiring physically separated.
  5. Add filtering only after identifying the dominant path.

50/60 Hz versus switching noise

Mains-related hum often appears as a low fundamental with harmonics. Switcher noise tends to produce higher-frequency whine or comb-like spectral features. An audio recording plus an SDR trace can distinguish the two quickly.

The rule for this project is simple: change one thing at a time and save before/after recordings. Troubleshooting becomes data rather than folklore.

Measure the improvement

Record ten seconds of received audio before every change and another ten seconds after it. Use the same receiver position and volume setting. A simple audio spectrum can show whether a 60 Hz component, its harmonics or a higher-frequency switching spur actually fell. This prevents a common troubleshooting trap: making several changes, hearing a small subjective improvement and not knowing which change was responsible.
